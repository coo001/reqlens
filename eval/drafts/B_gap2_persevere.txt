The bread oven at the bakery where I work starts at four in the morning. For most of my first year of community college, so did I.

In the spring of my senior year of high school, my father's hours at the warehouse were cut in half, and my family decided that I would take the early shift at a neighborhood bakery to cover our rent gap. I had already been accepted to college, and I refused to give that up. That meant shaping dough from four to nine, driving straight to an eight-thirty class I was always late for, and studying in the twenty minutes between the end of my shift and my next lecture. By October I had failed my first chemistry exam, and I seriously considered dropping out.

My grandmother, who had worked night shifts in a laundry for thirty years, called me every Sunday and quizzed me on the periodic table over the phone, even though she had never studied chemistry herself. In return I taught her how to video call my cousins abroad, and our Sunday calls became the best hour of both our weeks.

That year changed what I want to do with my life. I used to think of perseverance as a personality trait that some people simply have. Now I see it as a system: asking for help early, being honest about limits, and building routines that survive a bad week. I want to become a pharmacist, and I plan to work in a community pharmacy where many customers, like my family, juggle shift work and health. I know how much difference one flexible person can make, because my professor and my manager were those people for me.

I still bake on Saturdays, and I still volunteer every other Sunday at the food bank that helped my family that spring. The oven still starts at four. The difference is that I now know I can handle what comes after.
