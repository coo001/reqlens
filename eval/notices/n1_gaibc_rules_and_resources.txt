Event rules
Participation is free and open to students aged 13 and older who meet Devpost’s eligibility requirements and the restrictions stated on this page.
Participants may enter individually or in teams of up to six people total, including the submitter. Every team member must meet the eligibility requirements and be listed on the submission’s public Devpost team roster.
Participation is international subject to Devpost’s standard global eligibility exceptions. Devpost currently lists Brazil, Quebec, Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Crimea, Russia, and other restricted jurisdictions specified in its policy. Participants must also satisfy Devpost’s applicable account and consent requirements.
The event begins November 21, 2026, at 12:00 a.m. PST. Submissions are due November 28, 2026, at 3:00 p.m. PST. The time zone is America/Los_Angeles.
New projects and improvements to existing projects are permitted. Teams must disclose any existing work and clearly identify the additions or improvements completed during the hackathon. Judges will evaluate the finished project while focusing on the event’s contributions.
AI integration is encouraged but is not mandatory. AI-assisted development is permitted and can earn credit under the published rubric. Teams must accurately disclose the use of AI and distinguish their contributions from pre-existing tools, services, and other work.
Each submission must include a project description, a 2–4 minute demonstration video, public GitHub access to all project code, the complete team roster, a development disclosure, and an AI disclosure.
Each participant must submit their award or certificate name and contact email privately through the Participant Contact & Sponsor Opt-In form. Each team member fills out their own form. Sponsor opt-ins are optional.
Submissions must accurately represent the project’s behavior. Clearly disclose simulated, mocked, or unfinished features. Credit third-party work and comply with relevant licenses and Devpost’s terms.
Overall rankings use the full public 100-point judging rubric: Problem Value & Practicality 20 points, AI Integration & Development 30 points, Functionality & Reliability 30 points, and Design Quality & User Experience 20 points. Judges normally score the demo and project description, with supporting evidence where useful; installation or live use is not part of routine scoring. Organizers may verify the submitted version of leading projects or resolve technical questions. Judged category awards recognize the qualities described in their individual award descriptions.
Judges may use their judgment when applying the published criteria to different project types and deciding whether submitted evidence meets a checkpoint. The organizer may clarify wording or make minor adjustments to the judging process for fair and consistent scoring. Any significant change to the judging criteria or point values will be announced before the submission deadline.
Organizer’s Favorite Award is a discretionary selection by the organizer. Best Demo & Presentation is judged on clarity, structure, and how effectively the demonstration communicates the project’s value, capabilities, and limitations. Production budget and elaborate editing are not judging factors. Neither award requires AI integration, and neither changes overall scores or rankings.
The People’s Choice Award is determined by valid votes cast through Devpost during the public voting period. Voting runs from November 28, 2026, at 3:00 p.m. PST through December 1, 2026, at 3:00 p.m. PST (America/Los_Angeles). Public votes do not affect overall rankings or other judged awards.
Honorable Mentions recognize the five highest-scoring eligible projects under the published overall judging rubric that do not receive an overall placement or another category award. Each member of a recognized team will receive an Honorable Mention certificate.
A project may receive an overall placement and multiple category awards. All eligible participants will receive participation certificates. Winning team members will also receive certificates recognizing their overall placement or category awards. Any additional prizes will be announced only after they are confirmed.
Winners are scheduled to be announced on December 5, 2026, at 3:00 p.m. PST, within one week after the submission deadline.

Getting started
Start with a clear user and a manageable goal. Decide what a successful demonstration should show, then build toward that outcome.
If you are improving an existing project, document the starting point before making changes. Keep a concise record of the features, fixes, and other improvements completed during the event.
Keep your GitHub repository organized and provide instructions that help another person understand and run the project. Identify dependencies and external service requirements.
Document AI integrated into the product separately from AI used during development. Explain what each tool or model contributed and how your team reviewed its work.
Check the main task using examples the project should handle. Document relevant problems, the project’s response, and known limitations.
Prepare a 2–4 minute demonstration showing the actual project in use. Before submitting, check that your video and repository links work and that every teammate is listed.
Each participant must also complete the Participant Contact & Sponsor Opt-In form with their award or certificate name and contact email.
Review the complete judging rubric below and provide the evidence you want judges to consider. Use the demo for the actual project and the description for detailed explanations, comparisons, and test summaries.

How judges use this rubric
The rubric provides the general framework judges will follow. Participants are encouraged to focus on building projects that work well and serve their intended purpose. Use the rubric to explain your project’s strengths; you do not need to add features simply to match the examples.
Judges may use their judgment when applying the criteria to different project types and deciding whether the submitted evidence meets a checkpoint. Minor clarifications or adjustments to the judging process may be made to keep scoring fair and consistent. The published criteria and point values remain the basis for scoring. Each checkbox earns its printed points when the submission demonstrates the requirement; otherwise award zero. Score each checkbox independently. Missing a scoring item loses only its associated points and does not by itself make an entry ineligible.
Setup instructions, AI disclosures, a public GitHub repository, a complete team roster, and the required demo remain submission requirements. Simply supplying these items, naming a tool, stating a test goal, or recruiting an outside tester earns no separate quality bonus. Outside feedback may still support evidence of user need or benefit.
Judges normally score from the demo and project description. Screenshots, concise test summaries, cited sources, and linked supporting material may provide evidence. Judges do not normally install software, run code, or repeat tests. A hosted application and an outside tester are optional. Organizers may separately verify leading projects or resolve a specific technical question using the submitted version.
Use the required 2–4 minute video to show the actual project and its main task. Put detailed comparisons, AI decisions, evaluation evidence, test summaries, and design explanations in the description. The video does not need to answer every checkbox. Rubric labels such as A2 and R2 can help judges find the evidence.
The rubric applies across eligible project types, including software, models, games, hardware, research or analysis tools, and creative applications. Examples explain possible evidence, not required features. No particular AI provider, prompts, input-preparation method, custom training, paid tool, or number of AI components is required.
Problem Value & Practicality — 20 points
V1 — Is there a real need, and does the project address it? — 8 points
- ☐ Credible evidence supports a specific need or desired experience for the intended users. Evidence may be an observation, user comment, firsthand experience, published source, or concrete example. 4 points
- ☐ A realistic use example shows how the project’s main capability addresses the stated user need. Naming a problem is not enough; the connection between the project and the user’s goal must be clear. 4 points
Evaluate the proposed value here, assuming the described capabilities work. Actual working behavior is scored under R. Small audiences, entertainment, creativity, learning, accessibility, and personal usefulness are valid benefits. A real user’s account can support the need, but recruiting a user or repeating the same account earns no separate bonus.
V2 — Does the project offer a supported advantage? — 8 points
- ☐ The comparison uses a reasonable existing tool, manual method, or current lack of a solution for the same user goal. The team makes clear where the comparison facts came from. 3 points
- ☐ The comparison evidence supports a specific benefit for the user. For example, the project enables an activity the current approach cannot support, removes an existing inconvenience, or achieves a comparable result with less time or fewer resources. 5 points
A comparison may use documented capabilities, required steps, costs, user feedback, published information, or actual tests. Original head-to-head tests are optional. Simply saying the project is better, or listing unrelated differences, is insufficient.
V3 — Could the intended users realistically use the project? — 4 points
- ☐ The proposed use is workable for the intended users with the equipment, information, permissions, and operating resources the project needs. If a major requirement could block use, the team explains a feasible way to meet the requirement. 4 points
Judge the practicality of the proposed use, not whether the team wrote a long setup guide. A student tool should have a workable access route for students, for example. A public deployment or outside tester is not required.
AI Integration & Development — 30 points
A1 — Is the selected AI a justified choice? — 6 points
- ☐ The team explains why the particular AI model, tool, or method is a good choice for the project’s job, pointing to a relevant strength such as recognition quality, speed, cost, efficient resource use, or ability to work on the user’s device. 3 points
- ☐ Credible comparison information or test evidence supports the stated reason for the AI choice. The supporting difference must matter for the project; a generic claim that the selected AI is powerful does not qualify. 3 points
Official pricing, documented capabilities, relevant published test results, and the team’s own tests can support the choice. Original comparison tests are optional. Naming the AI tool is a required disclosure, not a separate scoring achievement.
A2 — Has the team made an effective project-specific AI choice? — 10 points
- ☐ The submitted project includes an implemented AI design choice addressing a particular project problem. The evidence shows the choice is actually part of the project, rather than planned work or merely the name of an AI service. 4 points
- ☐ Evidence demonstrates how the identified AI design choice helps meet the project’s goal. A worked example, relevant comparison, or test result must connect the choice to a concrete contribution, such as better task results, needed behavior, lower resource use, or a useful capability. 6 points
A1 scores which AI was selected; A2 scores how the selected AI is used. Possible design choices include data selection, model training, instructions, settings, connections to other project parts, or decisions about cost, speed, and privacy. These are alternatives, not a required feature list. No particular technique or number of AI components is required. A generally working feature alone does not establish why a specific AI design choice helps.
A3 — Has the team meaningfully checked AI quality and its limits? — 8 points
- ☐ The team shows a relevant check of the project’s AI work against a clear reference or requirement. The submission includes the actual results and explains the checking method. Merely stating an evaluation goal or promising future tests does not qualify. 4 points
- ☐ The team uses evaluation findings or relevant documented limitations to explain when the project’s AI results can and cannot be relied on. The explanation must concern the submitted project, rather than only saying that AI can make mistakes. 4 points
A3 scores the quality of the AI assessment and understanding of its limits. R scores the finished project’s observed results. Finding a weakness can support A3 even when the weakness loses points under R. The team does not have to invent a failure; a clear account of the tested scope and supported limits can qualify. Judges assess the supplied evidence without rerunning the assessment.
A4 — Was AI-assisted development checked by a person? — 6 points
- ☐ The team provides evidence of a person checking a specific AI contribution made during development. For example, generated code is tested against a known result, or generated material is checked against the project’s requirements. 3 points
- ☐ The team shows what the human check found and explains a resulting decision to keep, change, or reject the AI-created work. The decision is supported by the check result. 3 points
Simply reporting AI use earns no points. Reviewed AI work can qualify even if the human check finds no mistake. A1–A3 concern AI in the finished project and total 24 points; A4 concerns AI used to help build the project and totals 6 points. No particular provider, paid tool, or custom-trained model is preferred.
Functionality & Reliability — 30 points
R1 — Is the complete main process working? — 6 points
- ☐ The actual submitted project carries out the complete main process from its starting action to its final output. The demo shows the complete process and discloses any manual steps or special conditions needed for success. 6 points
This check concerns a complete working process. R2 checks whether the resulting outputs meet the project’s requirements. Planned interfaces and simulated results alone cannot establish working functionality. Judges normally assess the supplied demo and description rather than running the project.
R2 — Are the results correct across relevant situations? — 16 points
- ☐ A first ordinary example produces a result meeting the project’s stated requirements. 3 points
- ☐ A second ordinary example, using different information or a different situation, produces a result meeting the stated requirements. 3 points
- ☐ Repeating an ordinary example, or repeating a suitable check of its result, produces another result meeting the same requirements. 4 points
- ☐ A more demanding example within the project’s claimed capabilities produces a result meeting the stated requirements. 6 points
For every case, make clear what should happen and show what actually happened. A concise table with supporting screenshots, clips, or results is enough; four separate videos are not required. A demanding case might involve a large file, a difficult game or hardware situation, or another allowed condition harder than ordinary use. Cases can be predictions, analysis examples, creative outputs, or other appropriate project results. Variable outputs need not be identical if they meet the same requirements.
R3 — Are different kinds of failure handled safely? — 8 points
- ☐ A first demonstrated failure or unsupported situation is detected and handled through an appropriate correction, retry, backup, clear warning about an unsupported result, or clear end to the unsuccessful attempt. Earlier valid work is preserved where applicable. 4 points
- ☐ A second demonstrated failure with a different cause receives appropriate detection and handling under the same requirement, preserving earlier valid work where applicable. 4 points
Unreadable data and an unavailable service are different causes; two unreadable files usually represent the same cause. A failure can concern data, a model result, a component, a service, or a user action. Evidence may appear in the demo, screenshots, or a supported test summary. Judges do not need to trigger the failures themselves.
Design Quality & User Experience — 20 points
D1 — Is the main action or information easy to recognize? — 4 points
- ☐ The main actions and most important information clearly stand out throughout the main experience. The starting point, later steps, and results maintain a clear difference between primary content and optional details. 4 points
Judge the whole main experience as one design decision; do not award separate points for the same emphasis appearing on different screens.
D2 — Does the experience have a coherent, finished design? — 4 points
- ☐ Repeated parts follow a coordinated design throughout the demonstrated experience. Text styles, spacing, colors, controls, and labels work consistently, including relevant error or completion displays. 4 points
Judge consistent execution rather than a preferred visual style, expensive graphics, or decorative complexity. Extra screens or states that do not apply are not required.
D3 — Does the design remove unnecessary user effort? — 4 points
- ☐ A demonstrated design choice removes a specific user burden, such as repeated entry, avoidable navigation, or searching for a common action, while keeping necessary information and controls easy to reach. 4 points
A before-and-after walkthrough can support this check, but providing a walkthrough earns no separate point. The achievement is the actual reduction in user effort.
D4 — Does the presentation help users find and understand information? — 8 points
- ☐ Content is grouped, labeled, or ordered so users can find the information needed for their task. 3 points
- ☐ The presentation makes a useful relationship directly visible—for example, differences between options, the effect of a change, or the link between a finding and its supporting information. Users do not have to reconstruct that relationship from unorganized output. 5 points
Finding information and understanding its meaning are different checks. For example, labeled descriptions help users locate two options; matching details shown side by side help users compare the options. Apply the design checks to what users see and interact with, including software, games, hardware, commands, models, or analysis results. Judge the project’s design, not video production.
Judge support and score calculation
Each judge scores only the assigned section: 5 checks for V, 8 for A, 7 for R, or 5 for D. Judges should use what the submission demonstrates. If a checkbox requires unfamiliar technical knowledge, leave the checkbox pending and request a technical review instead of guessing. An unanswered evaluation is not a zero.
Add the checked points within each section. If several judges score the same section for a project, average their section totals. Add the four section averages: V + A + R + D = overall score out of 100. Do not apply the section weights a second time. Keep unrounded scores for ranking; round only the displayed result.
A project provisionally scoring 95 or above receives a second independent review of all four sections using the same published rubric and submitted evidence. Include the second reviewers’ scores in the section averages. Verification checks the submitted version and does not introduce new scoring criteria.
AI is encouraged, not mandatory. A1–A3 provide up to 24 points for AI used by the finished project. A4 provides up to 6 points for reviewed AI-assisted development. A project using no AI can earn up to 70 overall points; a project using AI only during development can earn up to 76.
For existing work, judges evaluate the finished submission while paying particular attention to the disclosed contributions completed during the hackathon. Overall scoring adds no separate points for team size, spending, video production, or presentation confidence. Category awards and public votes do not add points to overall rankings.
Scores are recorded using Devpost’s offline judging option. This describes score recording; the hackathon remains online.
